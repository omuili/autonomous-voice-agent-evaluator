# Architecture

I kept the live call path intentionally short. Twilio places the outbound call and uses Media Streams to exchange PCMU audio with my FastAPI backend over a persistent WebSocket. The backend forwards the incoming target-agent audio to OpenAI Realtime, where the patient simulator listens and responds in speech; the generated audio is sent back through the same FastAPI/Twilio path. I chose Realtime speech-to-speech instead of a sequential speech-to-text → language model → text-to-speech chain because the shorter path gives me better turn latency, voice activity detection, and practical barge-in handling during a real phone conversation.

I separated evidence collection and assurance from the critical audio path. Twilio creates a dual-channel recording after each call, which I diarize and convert into a timestamped transcript before running structured scenario evaluation and reviewing the final findings. In parallel, I instrumented the application with TraceVox, an AI observability and assurance platform I developed. Each call is represented as a TraceVox Autonomous Run with asynchronous lifecycle events, latency, speaker activity, interruption data, transcript/evaluation evidence, risk signals, and attributed findings. TraceVox is failure-isolated through background queues and retries, so observability can never block the phone conversation or the local recording/transcript/report pipeline.
